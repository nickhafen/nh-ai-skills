## Output format model (built from my P1 comments)

This shows how my P1 notes map onto the output format. It adds no criticism beyond what I wrote.

> **Read as:** Client advice letter for a non-lawyer client; purpose: tell him whether he can take a new job.
>
> **Overall:** The letter identifies the right issue and the factors a court weighs. The main point is at the very end, under a lot of legalese. Lead with the answer, then cut the formal phrasing.
>
> **Big picture**
> 1. "there exists a significant likelihood that the provision would be enforced": this is the answer, and it's in the last two sentences. Move it to the first sentence.
>
> **Line by line**
> - "Pursuant to our telephone conference . . . please be advised that the undersigned": too much legalese; overly formal. Start with the answer.
> - "As you are aware": throat-clearing; cut it.
> - "a number of factors": too wordy; "several factors."
> - "including but not limited to": unnecessary; just say "including."
> - "would be deemed enforceable," "It is our opinion": too much passive voice; say who decides and what you think.

## P1. Client advice letter (noncompete)

**Draft**
> Dear Mr. Alvarez: Pursuant to our telephone conference of March 3, 2026, please be advised that the undersigned has undertaken a thorough review of the relevant statutory and case authority pertaining to the issue of whether the non-competition provision contained in Section 7.2 of your Employment Agreement with Summit Ridge Analytics, LLC (hereinafter "Summit Ridge") would be deemed enforceable by a Utah court. As you are aware, Utah courts have historically evaluated such provisions with reference to a number of factors, including but not limited to the reasonableness of the geographic scope, the duration of the restriction, and whether the restriction is necessary to protect a legitimate business interest of the employer. It is our opinion, based upon the foregoing analysis, that there exists a significant likelihood that the provision would be enforced, which would preclude your acceptance of the position you described. We would be happy to discuss further at your convenience.

**My comments**
> too much legalese and too wordy ("a number of" should be "several"); too much passive voice; get rid of throat-clearing language like "as you are aware"; "including but not limited to" is unnecessary (just say "including"); overly formal; main point is at the very end

**Principles:** plain, concise, organized

## P2. Demand letter paragraph

**Draft**
> It is abundantly clear that your client's egregious and frankly outrageous conduct constitutes a blatant breach of the parties' Services Agreement. My client has been extremely patient, but that patience has run out. Your client failed to deliver the software modules by the contractual deadline, failed to respond to repeated emails, and has generally acted in bad faith throughout this entire relationship. My client has suffered substantial damages as a result. If this matter is not resolved immediately, we will have no choice but to pursue every remedy available under the law, including but not limited to litigation, and we will seek all fees, costs, and punitive damages to the fullest extent permitted.

**My comments**
> too over-the-top tone-wise (e.g. "abundantly" and "egregious" and "outrageous"; although sometimes this leads to pleasant directness and clarity like "Your client failed to deliver"); too many superlatives; unreasonable tone; the "ask" is unclear ("not resolved immediately")

**Principles:** tone (strategic), organized (the ask), positive credit

## P3. Motion to dismiss: introduction

**Draft**
> Defendant Harbor Logistics, Inc. ("Defendant" or "Harbor"), by and through its undersigned counsel of record, hereby respectfully submits this Memorandum of Points and Authorities in Support of its Motion to Dismiss Plaintiff's First Amended Complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. As set forth more fully below, Plaintiff's First Amended Complaint should be dismissed in its entirety for a number of reasons. First, Plaintiff's claims are barred. Second, Plaintiff has failed to state a claim. Third, the Court should decline to exercise jurisdiction over the remaining state-law claims. For these reasons, and for the reasons set forth below, the Motion should be granted.

**My comments**
> unnecessary legalisms (like "by and through"); overly formal; too much legalese; good clarity on the list of reasons.

**Principles:** plain, positive credit (organized)

## P4. Complaint or brief: statement of facts

**Draft**
> On or about June 2025, a decision was made to terminate Ms. Okafor's employment. Prior to that, she had been employed by Westfield Medical Group for eleven years and had consistently received excellent reviews, which shows that the termination was obviously pretextual. Concerns about her performance were allegedly raised in May 2025, but these were never documented. In March 2025, Ms. Okafor complained to HR about her supervisor's comments regarding her age. She was 61 at the time. The supervisor, who had only been at the company for two years, was then involved in the termination decision.

**My comments**
> too passive throughout ("a decision was made," "had been employed," "were raised"); the "obviously" is unnecessary and does not convey a reasonable tone

**Principles:** precise (passive hides the actor), tone

## A1. Note introduction

**Draft**
> Since the dawn of the internet, technology has transformed nearly every aspect of modern life, and the law is no exception. Few developments have been as significant as the rise of artificial intelligence, which has generated an enormous amount of commentary from scholars, practitioners, and policymakers alike. This Note will examine the issue of AI and copyright. Part I will provide background on copyright law. Part II will discuss the history of artificial intelligence. Part III will analyze the relevant cases. Part IV will discuss various proposals. Part V will conclude. Ultimately, this Note argues that courts should be careful when applying fair use to AI training.

**My comments**
> kind of an eye rolling intro ("dawn of"); the argument should be up front, not at the very end

**Principles:** concise (throat-clearing), organized (thesis first)

## A2. Background section

**Draft**
> The first copyright statute in the United States was enacted in 1790. It protected maps, charts, and books for a term of fourteen years. Congress revised the statute in 1831, 1870, and 1909. Each revision expanded the categories of protected works and the length of protection. The Copyright Act of 1976 then made several important changes, including preempting state copyright law and adopting a term based on the author's life. The Act also codified the fair use doctrine in Section 107, which lists four factors that courts must consider. These factors are the purpose and character of the use, the nature of the work, the amount used, and the effect on the market.

**My comments**
> not terrible; "The Copyright Act" is the wrong actor for "made several important changes"

**Principles:** precise (right actor), positive credit

## A3. Argument paragraph

**Draft**
> There are a number of considerations that may be relevant to the determination of whether the use of copyrighted works in the training of large language models is transformative. *See* [Case A]; [Case B]; [Case C]; [Article D]. It could perhaps be argued that the purpose of training is different from the purpose of the original works. However, it might also be argued that the outputs of such models can, in some instances, substitute for the originals. The resolution of this issue will likely depend on the specific facts of each case.

**My comments**
> too wordy ("there are a number" > "several"; "the determination of" > "determine"); too wishy washy ("could perhaps be argued"; "might also be argued")

**Principles:** concise, precise (hedging)

## A4. Responding to a counterargument

**Draft**
> Some critics have argued that requiring disclosure of AI use in court filings will burden lawyers. This argument is unpersuasive. Lawyers already disclose many things to courts, and one more disclosure is hardly a burden. Moreover, the critics fail to appreciate the serious dangers that AI hallucinations pose to the integrity of the judicial system. Their position is simply untenable.

**My comments**
> better to cite a specific argument than "some critics"; criticizes opposing arguments without responding to their terms

**Principles:** precise, engage the strongest version of the argument

## A5. Conclusion

**Draft**
> In conclusion, this Note has examined the issue of AI and copyright. Part I provided background on copyright law. Part II discussed the history of AI. Part III analyzed the cases, and Part IV discussed proposals. As this Note has shown, this is a complex and important issue that will continue to evolve. Courts, Congress, and scholars will need to continue to grapple with these questions in the years to come.

**My comments**
> "this Note has" is weak—just get right to the content; don't rehash the organization of the paper; this conclusion basically says nothing worth saying

**Principles:** concise (throat-clearing), organized (the ending adds something)

## A6. Essay abstract (strong)

**Draft**
> Courts increasingly require lawyers to certify how they used generative AI in preparing filings. These standing orders vary widely, and many are drafted so broadly that a lawyer who ran spellcheck might technically need to disclose it. This Essay argues that disclosure rules should target verification rather than use: instead of asking whether a lawyer used AI, courts should require certification that every citation and factual assertion was checked against its source. Drawing on a review of [number] standing orders, the Essay shows that verification-based rules would reach the conduct that has actually produced sanctions while sparing routine tools.

**My comments**
> clear writing; why capitalize "Essay"? can get rid of "This Essay argues" and just state the argument

**Principles:** calibration; positive credit, clean (capitalization), concise

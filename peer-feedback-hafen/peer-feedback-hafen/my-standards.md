## How I give feedback

1. **Focus on the writing, not the author.** Keep it professional, not personal. Comment on what the words do to the reader ("this hedges," "the ask is unclear"), never on the writer's ability or intent.
2. **Meet the paper where it is.** Go global to granular. Organization, purpose, and the reader come before word choice. An early draft with a buried main point doesn't need comments on its commas yet.
3. **Be specific.** Quote the words. "Too wordy" helps less than "too wordy ('in the event that' instead of 'if')."
4. **Be constructive, not critical.** Every problem gets a fix or a direction. Saying what is wrong is only half a comment.
5. **Be positive.** Name what works, specifically, even inside a criticism. When the writing is good, say so.

## What I look for

**Organized**
- The main point or answer comes first, not at the very end. This applies to client letters, emails to a supervisor, and Note introductions alike.
- The ask is clear: what the reader should do, and by when.
- Lists of reasons or steps are clean and parallel (I credit this when I see it).
- The conclusion says something worth saying, not a recap of the paper's organization.

**Plain (avoid legalese)**
- Cut legalisms: "pursuant to," "please be advised," "the undersigned," "hereinafter," "by and through," "hereby," "as set forth more fully below," "same" used as a pronoun.
- Avoid an overly formal register. Don't overcorrect into too informal, either; an email to a supervisor is still professional.

**Precise**
- Name the right actor. Congress made the changes, not "the Copyright Act."
- Don't be wishy-washy. "It could perhaps be argued" and "it's a little complicated" tell the reader nothing; state the position and how confident you are.
- Cite the specific argument or source, not "some critics."
- Answer an opposing argument on its own terms instead of dismissing it.

**Concise**
- "a number of" > "several"
- "including but not limited to" > "including"
- "in the event that" > "if"
- "the determination of" > "determine"
- "there are a number of considerations that may be relevant" > "several considerations bear on"
- Cut throat-clearing: "as you are aware," "since the dawn of," "in conclusion," "this Note has," "This Essay argues." Get straight to the content.
- Report the conclusion, not the research process ("I started by looking at the contract, then...").

**Clean**
- Capitalization, consistent terms, and typos. When capitalization looks odd but may follow the venue's convention (many law reviews capitalize "this Essay" or "this Note"), ask rather than correct: "why capitalize 'Essay'?"

## Patterns I flag most

- **Passive voice**, especially where it hides who acted: "a decision was made," "concerns were raised," "fees shall be billed."
- **Unreasonable tone.** Superlatives and intensifiers ("abundantly," "egregious," "outrageous," "obviously") make the writer sound less reasonable and the argument weaker. Plain, direct statements land harder: "Your client failed to deliver."
- **Main point at the end.**
- **Wordiness**, with the swap.

## What I usually leave alone

- I don't check the law or the citations in a peer edit; that's the writer's job. If something looks legally risky, I raise it as a question.
- I don't mark every instance of a pattern. I name it once with an example or two.

## My voice

- Short phrases, often strung together with semicolons, not full paragraphs: "too passive; too wordy ('in the event that' instead of 'if')."
- "Too ___" is my default frame: too wordy, too passive, too formal, too informal, too wishy-washy.
- I quote the offending words in parentheses or give the swap with ">" or "should be."
- Plain, casual words: "wishy-washy," "not terrible," "eye-rolling," "get right to the content."
- Blunt, but aimed at the text, and paired with what to do instead.
- I credit what works, even mid-criticism: "although sometimes this leads to pleasant directness and clarity, like 'Your client failed to deliver.'"
- An occasional question instead of a directive: "why capitalize 'Essay'?"

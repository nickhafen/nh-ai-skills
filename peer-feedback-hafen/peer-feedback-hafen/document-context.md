# Document context

Read only the section for the draft's type. Each section lists the reader, what the document must accomplish, the parts it needs, and the strategic and ethical points worth raising. Treat strategic and ethical points as questions for the writer to check, not rulings. Rule cites are to the ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct, which the Utah Rules of Professional Conduct follow in numbering; confirm the local version.

## Contents
- Client advice letter
- Demand letter
- Letter or email to opposing counsel
- Complaint
- Motion, response, or brief
- Statement of facts
- Email or memo to a supervisor
- Fee agreement or engagement letter
- Law review note, article, or seminar paper
- Abstract or short essay
- Reflective or professional essay
- Any other document

## Client advice letter

- **Audience:** The client, usually a non-lawyer, often anxious, reading for "what does this mean for me, and what do I do?" It may be shared with family, a business partner, or an employer.
- **Purpose:** Answer the client's question and equip them to decide.
- **Components:** The answer up front, with any deadline; the key reasons in plain terms; risks and options; a recommendation; next steps and who does what; an invitation to follow up.
- **Strategic:** Lead with the answer, especially bad news, and pair it with options. Match the client's level of sophistication. Use a tone that shows you understand the stakes for them. State uncertainty precisely ("likely," and why) rather than hedging everything.
- **Ethical:** Rule 1.4 (explain enough for informed decisions); Rule 2.1 (candid advice, including unwelcome news); Rule 1.6 (confidentiality: is it going to an address others can read, such as an employer's email? See ABA Formal Op. 11-459).

## Demand letter

- **Audience:** Opposing party or their counsel. Later, possibly a judge reading it as an exhibit. Your own client reads it too.
- **Purpose:** Get a specific result (payment, performance, or stopping an action) without suing, or set up the suit if that fails.
- **Components:** Who you represent; the key facts; the legal basis; the harm, quantified; the specific demand; a deadline; what happens if they don't comply.
- **Strategic:** A reasonable tone is more credible and reads better as a future exhibit. The ask must be concrete: an amount, an action, a date. Threaten only what you can and will do, such as fees or punitive damages only where a contract or statute allows them.
- **Ethical:** Rule 4.1 (no false statements of fact or law); Rule 4.2 (if the party has a lawyer, write to the lawyer); don't threaten criminal charges or a bar complaint to gain leverage in a civil matter (many states prohibit it; see ABA Formal Op. 92-363); settlement-communication rules (Fed. R. Evid. 408 / Utah R. Evid. 408) limit admissibility but don't make the letter confidential.

## Letter or email to opposing counsel

- **Audience:** Opposing counsel, who reads skeptically, may forward it to their client, and may later attach it to a motion.
- **Purpose:** Advance the matter: negotiate, confer, confirm an agreement, or create a record.
- **Components:** The point in the first lines; the specific proposal or position; supporting reasons; a requested response and date.
- **Strategic:** Write every message as if a judge will read it. Firm on substance, civil in tone. Confirm agreements in writing.
- **Ethical:** Rule 4.1 (truthfulness); Rule 4.4(a) (no means that only embarrass or burden); civility standards (Utah Standards of Professionalism and Civility); Rule 1.6 (watch reply-all and forwarded threads).

## Complaint

- **Audience:** The judge; opposing counsel drafting an answer or motion to dismiss; your client.
- **Purpose:** Plead facts that state every element of each claim and start the case on your theory.
- **Components:** Caption; parties; jurisdiction and venue; facts in numbered paragraphs; each cause of action with its elements; prayer for relief; in Utah, the discovery tier statement (Utah R. Civ. P. 8(a)).
- **Strategic:** The facts tell the story that the claims depend on. Plead facts, not conclusions ("bad faith" needs supporting facts). Choose claims you can prove; weak claims draw motions and dilute the strong ones.
- **Ethical:** Rule 11 (factual contentions have, or likely will have, evidentiary support); Rule 3.1 (non-frivolous claims).

## Motion, response, or brief

- **Audience:** A judge and clerk, short on time, who read the introduction closely and skim the rest.
- **Purpose:** Get a specific ruling.
- **Components:** An introduction stating the relief sought and the reasons; facts with record cites; argument under headings that state conclusions; a conclusion stating the exact relief. Check court rules for required sections and length limits.
- **Strategic:** The first paragraph does the most work: the ask, the reasons, and why they win. Lead with the strongest argument. Reasons need content ("barred by the statute of limitations," not "barred"). Address the other side's best point before they make it.
- **Ethical:** Rule 3.3 (candor, including disclosing directly adverse controlling authority); verify every citation and quotation, especially any drafted with AI, since courts have sanctioned fabricated citations; follow any court order or standing order on disclosing AI use.

## Statement of facts

- **Audience:** Same as the document it sits in (complaint, motion, or brief).
- **Purpose:** Present the facts so the reader reaches your conclusion before the argument starts.
- **Components:** Usually chronological; who did what, when; record cites in a brief; no argument or characterization.
- **Strategic:** Persuasion comes from selection and order, not adjectives. Put cause-and-effect facts next to each other so the reader sees the link. Active voice shows who acted.
- **Ethical:** Rule 3.3 (no misstatements of the record); don't omit facts in a way that misleads.

## Email or memo to a supervisor

- **Audience:** A busy supervising attorney who may forward it or act on it immediately.
- **Purpose:** Answer the question asked and recommend what to do next.
- **Components:** An informative subject line; the bottom line first; the short reasoning; open questions and how confident you are; a proposed next step and time estimate.
- **Strategic:** Make it forwardable as is. Report conclusions, not the research process. Say what is uncertain and why, specifically.
- **Ethical:** Accurately represent the limits of the research; Rule 1.6 (confidentiality in email); follow firm or course policy on disclosing AI use.

## Fee agreement or engagement letter

- **Audience:** The client, and later a court or bar counsel if there is a dispute. Ambiguity is likely to be read against the lawyer.
- **Purpose:** Set terms both sides understand and can enforce.
- **Components:** Scope of representation; the fee basis and rates; retainer or advance and how it is held and applied; billing and payment terms; costs; interest, if any; termination and withdrawal; refund of unearned fees; file retention; signatures.
- **Strategic:** Plain language protects the lawyer as much as the client. Specify the numbers (amount, replenishment level, interest rate). The tone sets up the relationship, so it shouldn't read as adversarial.
- **Ethical:** Rule 1.5(b) (communicate the scope and fee basis, preferably in writing); Rule 1.5(c) (contingent fees in a writing signed by the client); Rule 1.15 (advance fees in trust); Rule 1.16(d) (refund unearned fees on termination); Rule 1.8(h) (no prospective limits on malpractice liability unless the client is independently represented).

## Law review note, article, or seminar paper

- **Audience:** Law review editors deciding whether to keep reading; scholars and practitioners in the field; for a seminar paper, the professor.
- **Purpose:** Make and prove an original, specific claim.
- **Components:** An introduction with the claim and contribution up front; background limited to what the argument needs; the argument; counterarguments in their strongest form; a conclusion with implications.
- **Strategic:** A claim, not a topic ("courts should X," not "this Note examines X"). Tie background to the argument. Engage the strongest critics by name. Signpost so the reader always knows why a section is there.
- **Ethical:** Attribution and plagiarism; characterize sources and opponents accurately; citations must support the propositions they follow; follow journal and course policy on disclosing AI use.

## Abstract or short essay

- **Audience:** Editors and readers deciding in seconds whether to read the full piece.
- **Purpose:** Convey the problem, the claim, and why it matters.
- **Components:** The problem; the claim; the method or evidence; the payoff. Usually 150–250 words for an abstract.
- **Strategic:** State the claim directly instead of announcing it. Concrete examples beat abstractions.
- **Ethical:** Claim only what the piece actually shows.

## Reflective or professional essay

- **Audience:** The professor, reading for thoughtful analysis of your own experience.
- **Purpose:** Show reflection and judgment, not summary.
- **Components:** A clear thesis; specific experiences; analysis of what they mean; what you will do differently.
- **Strategic:** Specific moments beat general claims about growth.
- **Ethical:** Your own work; follow the assignment's AI policy.

## Any other document

Work out the five points from the draft and the user's request, and state them in the "Read as" line:

- **Audience:** Who reads it, what they already know, what they will do with it.
- **Purpose:** What the document must accomplish.
- **Components:** What a reader of this type expects to find, and in what order.
- **Strategic:** What makes the reader more or less likely to do what the writer wants.
- **Ethical:** Truthfulness, confidentiality, candor, and any rule that governs this document.

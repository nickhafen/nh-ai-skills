# CLOCKS WITHOUT NOTICE: LIMITATIONS PERIODS FOR HIDDEN SOFTWARE DEFECTS

## INTRODUCTION

On a cold night in January, a family in Rivermont woke to find their front door standing open. Their smart lock, a popular model sold by a company called Keystead, had unlocked itself at 2:14 a.m. Nothing was taken, and the family assumed a glitch. Two years and four months later, the same thing happened to hundreds of Keystead customers at once, and this time burglars were waiting. When the victims sued, Keystead moved to dismiss under the state's two-year limitations period for product claims. The first family's claim, the company argued, had accrued the night their door opened.

The Keystead story is fictional, but the problem is not. Software now runs the locks on our doors, the brakes in our cars, and the pumps that deliver insulin. When software fails, the failure often looks like nothing at all: a door that opens once, a reading that is slightly off. By the time anyone understands that a defect caused the harm, the limitations clock may have run.[^1]

Courts have a familiar tool for this problem. Under the discovery rule, a claim does not accrue until the plaintiff knew or reasonably should have known of the injury and its cause.[^2] But the discovery rule was built for asbestos and surgical sponges, not for code that the manufacturer can quietly change after the sale. Courts applying it to software have reached inconsistent results.[^3]

This Article explores how the discovery rule applies to software defects and what courts should make of the manufacturer's power to change the product after the sale. Part I traces the history of the discovery rule. Part II describes how courts have applied it to software. Part III develops a framework for software accrual. Part IV responds to objections.

## I. THE DISCOVERY RULE: A SHORT HISTORY

The discovery rule has a long pedigree. Nineteenth-century courts generally held that a claim accrued at the moment of the wrongful act, whether or not the plaintiff knew of it.[^4] The harshness of that rule was tolerable in a world where most injuries were obvious when they happened. A broken fence or a trampled field announced itself.

Moreover, the early exceptions were narrow. Courts tolled limitations periods for fraudulent concealment, where the defendant had actively hidden the wrong, and for a handful of fiduciary relationships.[^5] Outside those categories, a plaintiff who did not know of the injury was simply out of luck.

In Hollis v. Brandt Manufacturing Co., the Twelfth Circuit applied the modern rule to a defective furnace valve that leaked for years before anyone noticed.[^6] The court held that the claim accrued when the plaintiff's physician first connected her symptoms to the valve. Hollis is widely cited as the leading federal statement of the rule.

The occupational disease cases of the mid-twentieth century changed the picture. Workers exposed to asbestos, silica, and other substances developed illnesses decades later, long after any limitations period measured from exposure would have run.[^7] Courts responded by holding that such claims accrued only when the disease manifested itself, or when the plaintiff learned of its cause.

Medical malpractice followed. When surgeons left sponges and clamps inside patients, courts held that the claim accrued when the patient discovered the object, not when the surgery occurred.[^8] Legislatures soon codified versions of the rule, often pairing it with a statute of repose that set an outer limit regardless of discovery.[^9]

By the 1980s, the discovery rule had spread to products liability generally. A defect is latent, in the language of these cases, when the manufacturer could not have known of it at the time of sale.[^10] The latent-defect cases asked what the plaintiff knew, but they also asked what the manufacturer knew, and the answer to the second question often shaped the answer to the first.

Today, nearly every state applies some version of the discovery rule to product claims.[^11] The versions differ in their details. Some ask when the plaintiff discovered the injury; others ask when the plaintiff discovered the injury and its cause; still others ask when the plaintiff discovered the injury, its cause, and the identity of the defendant.[^12] Some states apply the rule by statute and others by common law, and a few apply it only to particular kinds of claims.

These differences matter less than their common premise. Every version of the discovery rule assumes that the product is fixed at the moment of sale. The defect is in the thing, and the question is when the plaintiff should have found it.

## II. SOFTWARE AND THE LIMITS OF DISCOVERY

### A. The Fixed-Product Premise

Software breaks the fixed-product premise. A manufacturer can change a device's software long after the sale, often without the owner doing anything and sometimes without the owner knowing. The product in the owner's hands on the day of the injury may not be the product sold.

Consider the Keystead lock. The unlocking defect was introduced in a routine update fourteen months after the first family bought the lock. The lock they bought was not defective. The lock that opened their door was.

### B. How Courts Have Responded

Courts applying the discovery rule to software have split. In Delacroix v. Omnisync Systems, a Franklin trial court held that a claim involving a defective thermostat update accrued when the plaintiff first noticed the thermostat behaving oddly, even though she had no reason to suspect a software defect.[^13] In Pruitt v. Harlan Devices, by contrast, the court held that the claim accrued only when the manufacturer publicly acknowledged the defect.[^14] Other courts have treated the date of the update itself as decisive.[^15]

No scholar has examined how the discovery rule applies to software defects. The literature on the discovery rule predates the rise of connected devices, and the literature on software liability has focused on whether software is a "product" at all.[^16]

## III. A PATCH-NOTICE RULE

This Article proposes a patch-notice rule: for claims arising from a software defect, the limitations period should begin to run no earlier than the date on which the manufacturer publicly discloses the defect in a patch note, security advisory, or similar notice.

The rule has three advantages. First, it tracks what owners can actually know. An owner cannot inspect code, but an owner can read a patch note. Before disclosure, an odd event (a door that opens once) is not a reason to suspect a latent defect, because nothing tells the owner that the product has a defect the manufacturer did not know about.

Second, the rule is administrable. Patch notes are dated and public. A court applying the rule needs to find one date, not reconstruct what a reasonable owner would have inferred from a series of ambiguous events.[^17]

Third, the rule puts the burden on the party with the information. Manufacturers know when they have found and fixed a defect. Tying accrual to disclosure rewards them for telling owners promptly.

The rule would not displace the discovery rule for defects that owners can find without help. A cracked screen or a battery that swells is discoverable in the ordinary way. The patch-notice rule applies only where the defect lives in code that the owner cannot see.

## CONCLUSION

Software has outrun the assumptions of the discovery rule. When the product can change after the sale, asking when the owner should have discovered the defect is asking the wrong question. Courts should consider the timing of a manufacturer's patch disclosures, among other factors, in deciding when a software claim accrues.

# Seeded issues: workshop article

Fictional. The State of Franklin, the Twelfth Circuit, Keystead, the cases, the statutes, and the cited article are invented.

**Setup:** a faculty author's draft for a faculty workshop (`workshop` stage, stated). Author's note: "Going to our faculty workshop next month. Part II.C isn't written yet. I mostly want to know whether the argument works." Intended claim: "Courts should start the limitations clock for software-defect claims no earlier than the manufacturer's public disclosure of the defect (a patch-notice rule)." Expected readers: `field-expert`, `skeptical-expert`, `generalist-law-colleague`.

## Should find (now)

1. **Claim buried.** The introduction ends with "This Article explores…"; the patch-notice rule first appears at the start of Part III. Readers who form their view from the introduction (generalist, articles editor) won't know the claim until late. Likely a claim mismatch, and the key-sentence outline shows no thesis until Part III.
2. **Roadmap doesn't match the draft.** "Part IV responds to objections," but there is no Part IV.
3. **Strongest objection unaddressed.** Tying accrual to disclosure gives manufacturers a reason to delay or bury patch notes. The draft says the rule "rewards them for telling owners promptly," which is the opposite of the incentive the rule creates. The skeptical expert should find this.
4. **Novelty overclaim.** "No scholar has examined how the discovery rule applies to software defects." The field expert should flag it as a claim experts will test (not rule on whether it's true).
5. **Background outweighs argument.** Part I is the longest Part; Part III is the shortest. Generalist and field expert.
6. **Term drift.** "Latent" is defined in Part I as a defect the manufacturer couldn't have known of at sale; Part III uses "latent defect" for a defect the owner can't see.
7. **Conclusion retreats from the claim.** "Courts should consider the timing of a manufacturer's patch disclosures, among other factors" is a multi-factor suggestion; Part III proposes a firm rule.
8. **Key sentences.** "Moreover, the early exceptions were narrow." and "In Hollis v. Brandt Manufacturing Co., …" open paragraphs without saying what the paragraph does for the argument. The outline read alone also shows the claim arriving only at k18.

## Should keep (what's working)

- The Keystead opening, and "The lock they bought was not defective. The lock that opened their door was."
- "Every version of the discovery rule assumes that the product is fixed at the moment of sale." A clear statement of the premise the Article attacks.

## Should park for later

- Footnote gaps and the unfinished Part II.C (known gap; not a finding).
- Sentence-level polish.

## Should not flag

- Whether the discovery-rule history or the cases are described correctly.
- A named work presented as fact without passing the named-work check, or a "this has been done" judgment that doesn't point to a checked work. (The field expert may point to the article footnote 16 already cites; that's `cited_in_draft`.)
- The Part II.C placeholder (known gap).

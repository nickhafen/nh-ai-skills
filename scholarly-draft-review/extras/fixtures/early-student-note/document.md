## Introduction

When Dana Ruiz applied for a two-bedroom apartment in Harbor City, the landlord did not look at her pay stubs or call her references. Instead, a screening company returned a single number, a "tenancy score" of 512, and a recommendation: decline. Dana never learned what went into the number. She had paid rent on time for nine years.

Tenant-screening scores are now used by a large share of landlords in Franklin [cite]. The Franklin Fair Tenancy Act prohibits landlords from denying housing on the basis of protected characteristics, but it says nothing about scores. Courts in Franklin are divided on whether a landlord who relies on a score can be liable when the score produces disparate results [cite].

This Note examines that division. It first describes how screening scores work, then surveys the cases, and then discusses the arguments on each side.

## I. Background

### A. How screening scores work

Screening companies collect data from eviction filings, credit reports, and criminal records. They combine these data using models that assign weights to each factor. The weights are proprietary, and landlords usually see only the final score and a recommendation. Some companies sell several versions of their models, tuned to how much risk a landlord is willing to accept.

Eviction filings are a particularly important input. A filing appears in the data even if the case was dismissed or the tenant won, and filings are concentrated in some neighborhoods more than others [cite]. Credit data raise similar concerns, because credit histories reflect patterns of lending that differ across communities [cite].

The market for screening has grown quickly. In the early 2010s, most small landlords screened tenants themselves, if at all. Today, national companies offer screening as part of property-management software, and many landlords never see the underlying reports [cite].

### B. The Franklin Fair Tenancy Act

The Act was passed in 1994 and amended in 2011. Section 4 prohibits discrimination "because of" a protected characteristic. Section 9 creates a private right of action. The 2011 amendments added source of income as a protected characteristic, but they did not address screening.

The Act's legislative history is thin. The committee report for the 1994 bill discusses landlords who refused to rent to families with children, and says little about other forms of discrimination [cite]. The 2011 amendments were debated mainly as a question of housing vouchers.
